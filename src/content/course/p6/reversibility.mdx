<Analogy>
  <p>A door you can walk back through, and a door that locks behind you. Most doors are the first kind, and people treat them like the second — standing in the corridor deliberating about a room they could simply step into and leave.</p>
</Analogy>

## One question, applied everywhere

**If this turns out to be wrong, what does it cost to undo?**

That question decides how much thought a decision deserves, how much evidence it needs, and how safely it can be delegated to an agent. It is the most portable piece of judgment in this course.

| | Reversible | Irreversible |
|---|---|---|
| **Examples** | A library choice, a function name, a UI layout, a caching strategy | Your data model, a public API, a stored data format, deleting data, a vendor with your data |
| **Cost to undo** | Hours | Weeks, or impossible |
| **Deserves** | A quick decision, tried in practice | An ADR, an adversarial review, a night's sleep |
| **Delegation** | Let the agent decide and tell you | You decide; the agent implements |

## The two failures, and one is much more common

**Over-deliberating a reversible decision** is the everyday one: two days comparing libraries you could have swapped in an afternoon. The cost is invisible because it looks like diligence.

**Under-deliberating an irreversible one** is rarer and far more expensive: a data model chosen in ten minutes that shapes three years of work.

Agents amplify both. They will happily produce a detailed comparison matrix for a trivial choice, and they will also make a load-bearing schema decision inside a `/develop` run without flagging it — because nothing told them which was which.

<Callout type="tip" label="Put it in AGENTS.md">
  <p>One line, and it changes how every agent works on your project:</p>
  <p><i>"Decisions that change stored data, a public interface or anything a user can see the history of are irreversible. Stop and ask before making one, even mid-task."</i></p>
</Callout>

## What makes something irreversible, in practice

It is rarely the code. Four things:

1. **Data written in a format.** Once a million rows use it, the format is a contract with the past.
2. **An interface someone else depends on.** Published API, event schema, a URL people bookmarked.
3. **Anything users have seen.** Removing a feature people rely on is technically easy and socially expensive.
4. **Deletion.** Data you did not keep cannot be recovered by any amount of later cleverness.

Notice that none of these is "we wrote a lot of code". Volume of code feels like commitment and is usually the cheapest thing to throw away.

## Make irreversible things reversible, when you can

The best move is often to change the category:

- **Expand, migrate, contract** (A3.5) turns a destructive migration into a reversible sequence.
- **Feature flags** turn a release into a switch.
- **Soft deletes** turn deletion into a state change, at least for a while.
- **Versioned APIs** let an old shape live alongside a new one.
- **Keeping the raw data** lets you recompute derived data when you learn you derived it wrongly.

That last one is underrated. Storing raw events and computing summaries from them means a mistake in your summary logic is an afternoon, not an archaeology project.

<Exercise id="rev-1" title="Sort your last twenty decisions" time="40 min" predict="What share of your deliberation went to reversible choices?">

1. List the last twenty technical decisions on your current project.
2. Mark each reversible or irreversible, and write the cost to undo.
3. Now mark how long you actually spent on each.
4. Find the mismatches — reversible decisions that took hours, irreversible ones that took minutes.
5. For every irreversible one with no ADR, write one now, including what would make you change it.

</Exercise>

<Expect>

Most people find the ratio inverted: hours on library choices, minutes on the schema. The exercise is uncomfortable in a useful way, and the ADRs you write in step 5 are usually the most valuable documents on the project.

</Expect>
