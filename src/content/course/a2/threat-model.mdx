## Why before, not after

Security designed in costs a little. Security added after a breach costs a lot, and some mistakes can't be undone: a leaked password database stays leaked. A **threat model** is a short, structured look at how your system could be attacked, done while the design is still on paper. Ten minutes for a small feature; an hour for a password manager.

## Four questions

1. **What are we protecting?** The **assets**: user data, passwords, money, tokens, your signing keys, your reputation.
2. **Who might attack, and how?** The **actors**: a curious user, a determined attacker, a compromised dependency, a malicious file your agent reads.
3. **Where can they get in?** The **trust boundaries** (lesson A1.1): every place data crosses from something you don't control to something you do.
4. **What stops them, and what if that fails?** The mitigations, and the damage if one fails.

## STRIDE, a checklist for question 2

Microsoft's STRIDE gives you six categories to walk through at each boundary, so you don't rely on inspiration:

| Letter | Threat | Question to ask | Typical defence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S** | Spoofing | Can someone pretend to be another user? | Strong authentication |
| **T** | Tampering | Can someone change data they shouldn't? | Authorization checks, integrity checks |
| **R** | Repudiation | Can someone deny doing something? | Audit logs |
| **I** | Information disclosure | Can someone see data they shouldn't? | Access control, encryption |
| **D** | Denial of service | Can someone make it unavailable? | Rate limits, quotas |
| **E** | Elevation of privilege | Can someone gain powers they shouldn't have? | Least privilege, role checks |

You don't need to be exhaustive. The goal is to find the few serious threats early, and make an explicit decision about each: mitigate it, accept it, or change the design.

## Don't forget the agent itself

From the OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications (December 2025): your *agents* are also part of the threat model. Goal hijacking through prompt injection, tool misuse, abuse of the agent's identity and permissions, and supply-chain attacks through packages, skills or MCP servers. When an agent works on a security-sensitive product, its access is an asset too.

<Exercise id="tm-1" title="Threat-model Vault before it exists" time="40 min" predict="What will turn out to be the single most valuable asset in a password manager, and the most likely attack?">

Project L2 is Vault, a zero-knowledge password manager. Threat-model it now, on paper, before any design.

1. Fill in the template for Vault: assets, actors, trust boundaries, and at least one STRIDE threat per boundary.
2. For each threat, choose: mitigate (how?), accept (why?), or redesign.
3. Ask your agent to attack your model: *"Here's my threat model for a zero-knowledge password manager. Act as an attacker: find the three most damaging attacks I missed, and rank my listed threats by real-world likelihood. Explain each."*
4. Keep the result. You'll use it in lesson A2.3 and in Project L2.

</Exercise>

<Expect>

The master password and the vault contents as the top assets; credential stuffing, a stolen phone, a malicious dependency, and a compromised server as leading threats. The agent will likely raise something you missed: recovery flows ("forgot master password"), or what the server logs.

</Expect>

  <BreakPart label="What happens">

  A team designs and builds a feature, then asks for a security review a week before launch. The review finds that the data model stores what it shouldn't, and fixing it means redesigning three screens and migrating data. Launch slips a month.

  </BreakPart>
  <BreakPart label="The fix">
