<Analogy>
  <p>Building one shop with two front doors, or two shops on the same street. Both work. One costs less to run and always feels slightly like a compromise at one of the doors.</p>
</Analogy>

## The honest comparison

| | Native per platform | Cross-platform | Web / PWA |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Feel** | Best; platform conventions for free | Very good; occasional seams | Adequate; clearly a website on iOS |
| **Cost** | Highest — two codebases, two skill sets | One codebase, some platform work | Lowest |
| **Platform features** | Immediate access | Waits for a plugin, or you write one | Limited; notifications and background work are weakest |
| **Distribution** | App stores, with review | App stores, with review | Instant; no review, no store cut |
| **Hiring** | Two specialisms | One | Easiest |

By lesson 6.1's test this is **near-irreversible**: it shapes your team, your hiring and your UI code for years. It deserves an ADR and a night's sleep.

## The questions that actually decide it

1. **What does the product need from the device?** Background location, Bluetooth, deep camera work and widgets push towards native. Forms, lists and simple media do not.
2. **Who is building it, for the next two years?** One person maintaining two native codebases is two half-maintained apps.
3. **How often must both platforms change together?** Frequent lockstep changes favour one codebase.
4. **Does it need to be an app at all?** A great many products are a website people bookmark. Ask before accepting the premise.

Note what is absent: raw performance. For the overwhelming majority of products, cross-platform performance is indistinguishable from native, and where it is not, the difference is usually in one screen you can write natively.

<Callout type="tip" label="The hybrid that usually wins">
  <p>Cross-platform for the app, with native modules for the two or three things that genuinely need them. You get one codebase for the 95% that is lists, forms and navigation, and full platform access exactly where the product demands it — without paying for two codebases everywhere else.</p>
</Callout>

## What agents changed here

Less than people expect. An agent writing Swift and Kotlin does not halve the cost of two native codebases, because the expensive part was never typing — it is two sets of conventions to review, two release processes, two sets of platform bugs, and twice the surface where a change can be forgotten in one place.

Where agents genuinely help: **keeping two codebases in step**. "Implement this feature in the Kotlin app to match what we just did in Swift, following the existing conventions there" is a task agents are good at, and it reduces the drift that makes two codebases painful. That moves the needle, but it does not change the category.

## Releases are the part people forget

Server code deploys in a minute. An app takes review time and then lives on users' phones for months (A3.5's L3 milestone). Decide before you ship:

- **How many app versions the API supports**, and enforce it in contract tests.
- **How to force an update** when a version is genuinely broken.
- **How to turn a feature off remotely**, because you cannot recall a release.

<Exercise id="plat-1" title="Decide it properly, once" time="50 min" predict="Does your product actually need to be an app?">

1. List everything your product needs from the device. Mark each essential or convenient.
2. Answer the four questions honestly, especially the second.
3. Write the trade-off format from 6.3, including the falsifier.
4. Ask an agent for options and which constraint is doing the most work — without stating your preference.
5. Write the ADR, including the release policy.

</Exercise>

<Expect>

The "does it need to be an app" question is the one that surprises people. A meaningful share of products that were built as apps needed a fast website plus push notifications — and the app existed because nobody asked the question at the start.

</Expect>
