<Analogy>
  <p>An editor who did not write the article. The writer reads what they meant to write; the editor reads what is actually on the page.</p>
</Analogy>

## Fresh context is the whole trick

A reviewer that shares the builder's context shares its blind spots. It has already been persuaded by the same reasoning. So review with **fresh context**, and ideally a **different model family** — a second opinion from the same model that just wrote the code is closer to a first opinion repeated.

Kun Chen's no-mistakes gate does exactly this: a different model re-examines every change, adversarially, before anything reaches the shared repo.

## Build it in layers

1. **The agent's own checks** — `scripts/check.sh` before it claims to be done.
2. **A fresh AI review on every push** — a `code-reviewer` subagent, a dedicated review command, or a gate remote.
3. **An optional pull-request bot** for comments in the usual place.
4. **A human** on sensitive paths: auth, payments, encryption, infrastructure.

Each layer catches what the one before misses, and each costs more than the last. That ordering is the point.

## What to actually expect

Numbers keep expectations honest. Kun measured his gate across about **1,000 agent changes in 59 repositories**: roughly **63% had at least one mistake caught** before a human looked.

Independent tests of pull-request bots show the trade-off between catching and noise. CodeRabbit caught about **46%** of seeded bugs with about **2 false alarms** per run; Greptile catches more — **82%** in its own benchmark — at around **11 false alarms**.

<Callout type="note" label="The first month is noisy">
  <p>Expect <b>15–30% of comments to be noise</b> at first, falling under 10% once you exclude generated files, fixtures and vendored code. If you do not exclude those, people stop reading the gate, and then the gate is just latency.</p>
</Callout>

<Exercise id="gate-1" title="Put a gate in front of your repo and measure it" time="40 min" predict="Out of your next ten changes, how many do you think the gate will find something real in? Write the number down.">

1. If you use an install script for a gate, **read it before running it**. Piping a script from the internet into your shell is exactly the risk you guarded against in Part 1.
2. Install your gate — the `agent-gate` role from your kit is a good starting point — and wire it so it runs on every push.
3. Push your next change through it and read what it reports.
4. For your next **ten** changes, tally each finding as one of three: **real issue**, **noise**, or **a bug it missed** that you found later.

</Exercise>

<Expect>

Real catches on some changes, some noise, and — most valuable — a clear picture of which mistakes *your* agents make most. That list is what you tune the gate and your AGENTS.md against. Most people's tally lands somewhere near Kun's: something real in roughly half to two-thirds of changes.

</Expect>

<IfNot>

If the gate finds nothing across ten changes, it is probably reviewing the wrong thing — check it is diffing against `main` and not against your last commit. If it finds something on every change and none of it matters, your exclusions are missing: start by excluding lockfiles, generated clients and fixtures.

</IfNot>

<Breaks title="How it breaks in real life">
  <BreakPart label="What happens">
    Review fatigue. The gate comments on everything, people start merging without reading it, and three weeks later a real finding sails through under twenty cosmetic ones.
  </BreakPart>
  <BreakPart label="Why">
    A reviewer with no threshold is indistinguishable from noise. Humans learn to ignore channels that are usually wrong, and they learn it fast.
  </BreakPart>
  <BreakPart label="The fix">
    Give the gate a severity threshold and a scope. "Report correctness, security and contract drift. Do not report style, naming or formatting — the linter owns those." Then measure the noise rate monthly; if it climbs above 10%, tighten again.
  </BreakPart>
</Breaks>
