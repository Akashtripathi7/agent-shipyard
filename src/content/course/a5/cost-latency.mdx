<Analogy>
  <p>A taxi meter. Every feature you add is another minute running. You would not design a route without knowing the fare — and you certainly would not let the passenger choose an unlimited one.</p>
</Analogy>

## Two budgets, decided before you build

Write these numbers down first, the same way A4.4 made you write a latency budget:

- **Cost per request**, and per user per day. Work back from what the feature is worth: if a support answer saves five minutes of staff time, it can justify a few pence — and not more.
- **Latency budget.** What the user is waiting for, end to end, at p95.

Both come from the product, not from the technology.

## Where the cost actually goes

Usually not where people assume. The largest input is almost always **the context you send**, not the answer you get back:

| Lever | Typical effect | Cost to you |
|---|---|---|
| **Send fewer retrieved chunks** | Large | Free; often *improves* quality |
| **Use a smaller model for easy cases** | Large | Routing logic and an eval set to prove it is safe |
| **Cache repeated prefixes** | Large on chatty systems | Minor plumbing |
| **Cache whole answers to common questions** | Very large | Invalidation, as in A3.4 |
| **Shorten the system prompt** | Moderate | Some rewriting |
| **Reduce maximum output length** | Small to moderate | Product decision |

**Routing is the biggest structural win.** Most questions are easy. Classify cheaply, send the easy ones to a small fast model, escalate the rest — and prove with your eval set that the small model is adequate for the class you route to it, rather than assuming.

## Latency is a product problem

You cannot make a model instant, so design the wait:

- **Stream the response.** The perceived wait is time-to-first-token, not total time, and streaming often changes an unacceptable feature into a comfortable one without making anything faster.
- **Do retrieval in parallel** with anything else you need.
- **Say what is happening.** "Looking through your booking history…" beats a spinner, and it is honest.
- **Have a timeout and a fallback** — a handover to a human, or a cached answer. A provider outage must degrade the feature, not the product.

## Watch the right numbers

Per feature, tracked like any other service (A4.6): **cost per request**, **tokens in and out**, **p50/p95/p99 latency**, **cache hit rate**, **error and timeout rate**, and **fallback rate**.

Alert on cost per user per day exceeding the budget, and on fallback rate climbing — a rising fallback rate is usually the first sign that something upstream is unwell.

<Callout type="warn" label="The runaway loop">
  <p>An agent loop with no iteration cap and a tool that can fail will retry until something stops it. In development that is an afternoon's spend; in production, with concurrent users, it is a bill that arrives before the alert does. <b>Cap iterations, cap tokens per request, cap spend per user per day</b> — all three, in code.</p>
</Callout>

<Exercise id="a5c-1" title="Halve the cost without losing quality" time="55 min" predict="Which lever will give the biggest reduction? Guess before measuring.">

1. Instrument your feature: tokens in, tokens out, cost and latency per request.
2. Run your eval set and record baseline cost, p95 latency and quality score.
3. Apply one lever at a time, re-running the evals after each: fewer chunks; prefix caching; routing easy cases to a smaller model.
4. Plot cost against quality after each change.
5. Stop at the point where quality starts to fall.

</Exercise>

<Expect>

Sending fewer retrieved chunks usually cuts cost substantially **and** improves quality, because irrelevant context distracts the model. That is the counter-intuitive result worth internalising: for retrieval, more context is often worse as well as more expensive.

</Expect>

<IfNot>

If quality fell as soon as you cut chunks, your reranking is weak — you are relying on quantity to make sure the right passage is somewhere in the pile. Fix ranking first, then cut.

</IfNot>
