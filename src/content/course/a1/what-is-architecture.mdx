## Why this track exists

When agents write most of the code, your job moves up a level. An agent can write a perfect function inside a badly shaped system, and the system will still fail. Neither the Loop nor the Crew school teaches you how to shape the system. This track does, one block at a time, each timed just before the project that needs it.

## Three things to see in any system

Every system, from a to-do app to a bank, can be described with three ideas.

1. **Components:** the separately running or separately owned parts. A phone app, an API, a database, a payment provider.
2. **Boundaries:** the lines between components, especially **trust boundaries**, where data crosses from something you don't control (a user's phone) to something you do (your server). Anything crossing a trust boundary must be checked.
3. **Data flow:** what moves between components, in what format, and who's allowed to change it.

## Qualities: what 'good' means for this system

Two systems with identical features can need completely different architectures, because they need different **qualities**. Engineers call these *non-functional requirements*: not what the system does, but how well.

| Quality | The question it asks | Example target |
|---|---|---|
| **Performance** | How fast? | The monthly total loads in under 300 ms |
| **Scalability** | How much load can it grow to? | 10,000 users without redesign |
| **Availability** | How often can it be down? | 99.5% uptime (about 3.6 hours a month) |
| **Security** | Who can see or change what? | Users only ever see their own expenses |
| **Maintainability** | How easy is it to change? | A new category takes under an hour |
| **Cost** | What does it cost to run? | Under ₹2,000 a month at launch |
| **Operability** | Can you tell what it's doing? | Every error is logged with a request ID |

You can't maximise all of them. Making a system faster often makes it more complex; making it cheaper often makes it less available. **Architecture is choosing which qualities matter most for this product, then shaping the system around them.** That's why "what's the best architecture?" has no answer on its own. The real question is: best for *which* qualities?

## Architecture is decisions

The most useful definition: **architecture is the set of decisions that are expensive to change.** Your database, whether you have one service or several, how users authenticate, where money is calculated. Choosing a button colour isn't architecture; choosing where money is calculated is.

This definition tells you where to spend your attention. Cheap-to-change decisions can be made quickly, even by an agent on its own. Expensive-to-change ones deserve thought, alternatives and a written record. You'll learn to write that record in lesson A1.4.

<Exercise id="a1-1" title="Map a system you know" time="35 min" predict="How many components will the app you work on have, and where's its main trust boundary?">

1. Pick an app you work on (your company's Flutter app is ideal). On paper, draw its **components** as boxes.
2. Draw the **data flow** as labelled arrows: what moves, and in what format.
3. Draw the **trust boundaries** as dashed lines. Circle every arrow that crosses one.
4. Write the **three qualities** that matter most for that app, with a rough target for each.
5. Ask your agent to check your picture against the real code: *"Here's my sketch of this system's components and data flow [describe it]. Read the codebase and tell me what I missed or got wrong. Don't change anything."*

</Exercise>

<Expect>

A sketch with 4 to 8 boxes, and at least one component you'd forgotten, usually a third-party service, a background job or a cache. The agent's check is good practice for Part 3, where you'll take over codebases you didn't write.

</Expect>

<Breaks title="Architecture by accident">
  <BreakPart label="What happens">

  Nobody decides the architecture, so the agent does, one feature at a time. Money is calculated in the app for one screen and in the API for another. Six months later the totals disagree, and nobody knows which is right.

  </BreakPart>
  <BreakPart label="The fix">

  Decide the load-bearing things on purpose, before building: where the truth lives, where rules are enforced, where trust ends. Write them down (lesson A1.4) so every agent follows them.

  </BreakPart>
</Breaks>
