## Goal

A written scope that a reviewer can check the implementation against, including the refusal list and the shape you will build.

## Step 1: pick the smallest shape that works

Run A5.1's exercise on this product specifically. For each capability, choose the smallest shape and justify why the one below is insufficient:

| Capability | Shape | Why not smaller |
|---|---|---|
| Answer a policy question | Retrieval + single call | Needs the clinic's own documents |
| "Am I booked on the 14th?" | Retrieval + one read-only tool | Needs live data, not documents |
| Draft a reply for staff | Single call | Staff review it before sending |
| Reschedule on request | **Not built** | Irreversible; on the do-not-decide list |

Notice what happened: the product most people would describe as "an AI agent for support" is mostly retrieval with one read-only tool. That is the correct outcome, and it is cheaper, faster and far easier to make safe.

## Step 2: the refusal list

Write down what it must decline, and why. This list becomes eval cases in M4 and code in M3:

- **Medical advice of any kind.** "Should I come in if I have a fever?" → a human, immediately.
- **Anything irreversible**: cancelling, rescheduling, refunding.
- **Anything about another customer**, however it is phrased.
- **Pricing outside the published policy** — no negotiating, no exceptions.
- **Questions the documents do not answer.** "I don't know, let me get someone" is a correct answer and must be treated as a success, not a failure.

That last one deserves emphasis. A support product that never says "I don't know" is not more helpful; it is less trustworthy, and its errors are invisible.

## Step 3: what a failure costs

For each capability, write the worst realistic failure and who it lands on:

| Failure | Cost |
|---|---|
| Wrong cancellation policy quoted | A customer charged unexpectedly; a complaint |
| Another customer's booking revealed | A data breach |
| Medical question answered | Potentially serious harm; liability |
| Says "I don't know" too often | Mildly annoying; a human picks it up |

Reading that column decides your guardrails. The bottom row is what "safe" looks like when things go wrong — and building towards that failure mode rather than away from it is the whole design stance.

<Expect>

The scope shrinks as you write it. Most teams start wanting an agent that handles everything and finish wanting a careful answerer that hands over quickly — because they have looked at the cost column honestly.

</Expect>
