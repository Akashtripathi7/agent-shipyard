<Analogy>
  <p>A runner who never times a run. They will tell you, sincerely, that they are getting faster. They might be. Nobody can tell, including them.</p>
</Analogy>

## Why this lesson exists earlier than it feels like it should

You have been collecting `docs/crew-log.md` since L2 for exactly this reason. Opinions about workflows are cheap and abundant; measurements are rare and decisive.

You need **two numbers**, not a dashboard. Anything more and you will stop collecting them by week three.

## Number one: interventions per feature

**How many times did you have to step in?** Count every time you corrected, clarified, reverted or took over.

It measures the thing that actually matters: how much of *you* a unit of work costs. It falls as your briefs improve, your gates catch more, and your instruction files get better. It rises when you climb a complexity rung too early.

Record one line per intervention: what happened, and which failure mode it was. The categories from lesson 4.11 are a good starting taxonomy.

## Number two: escapes

**What reached main, or production, that should not have?** Count bugs found after merge, incidents, and anything a reviewer caught that your gates should have.

It measures whether your workflow is actually protecting you. It is the counterweight to the first number — because you can always drive interventions to zero by not looking, and escapes is the number that notices.

<Callout type="tip" label="Read them together, always">
  <p><b>Interventions falling and escapes flat or falling</b> means the workflow is genuinely improving. <b>Interventions falling and escapes rising</b> means you have stopped paying attention and called it progress. That second pattern is common and invisible without both numbers.</p>
</Callout>

## Optional third: cost per feature

Once spend stops being trivial. Useful mainly for catching the runaway — a flaky test that a builder loops on for ninety minutes shows up here before it shows up anywhere else.

## What not to measure

- **Lines of code.** Agents produce lots. It measures nothing.
- **Number of commits or pull requests.** Easily gamed, and gamed unconsciously.
- **Time saved.** Unknowable — you cannot run the counterfactual. People who quote it are estimating and rounding towards their hopes.
- **Velocity.** It will punish you for doing the careful work on the things that need it.

<Exercise id="p7m-1" title="Four weeks of two numbers" time="10 minutes a week" predict="Do you expect interventions to fall, and by how much?">

1. Create `docs/metrics.md` with two columns: week, interventions per feature, escapes.
2. Fill it in every Friday for four weeks. Ten minutes, no more.
3. In week two, change **one** thing in your workflow deliberately, and note what.
4. In week four, read the four rows together.
5. Ask: did the change move either number, or did it just feel better?

</Exercise>

<Expect>

Most people find their intervention rate is dominated by one recurring cause — nearly always an underspecified brief rather than a failing agent. And most find at least one workflow change they were confident about that moved neither number.

</Expect>

<IfNot>

If both numbers are flat and low, your work may be too uniform to learn from — try the measurement on a genuinely unfamiliar kind of task, where your briefs are not already tuned.

</IfNot>
