<Analogy>
  <p>A cook who has worked in three kitchens and now writes the service manual for their own. Not by inventing everything, and not by copying one kitchen wholesale, but by keeping what worked and cutting what was someone else's problem.</p>

## Neither workflow was designed for you

You have now run two published workflows — the feature loop and the crew model. Neither was built for your projects, your review speed, your risk tolerance, or the fact that you work alone on some things and with people on others.

This part turns them into yours. Four steps, one page of output.

## Step 1: keep what you actually used

Go through the stages honestly. Which did you run every time? Which did you skip and never regret?

**A stage you always skip is either wrong for your work or badly automated**, and the answer is different for each:

- *Wrong for your work* — you never write ADRs on throwaway projects, and that is correct. Cut it for that project type.
- *Badly automated* — you skip verification because it takes twenty minutes of manual work. Do not cut it; fix it.

Telling these apart is the whole exercise. The test: **would you be upset to discover it had been skipped on something that mattered?** If yes, it is badly automated. If no, cut it.

## Step 2: turn repeated instructions into skills

Anything you have typed more than twice is a skill. Most people end up with four to six: one to start a feature, one to review, one to ship, one to debug, and one that encodes their own product's rules.

The test of a good skill is that you stop thinking about its wording. If you still edit it every time you use it, it is not finished — it is a template, and the parts you keep editing belong as parameters rather than prose.

## Step 3: decide what is law

For each rule, decide: a **request** in an instruction file, or a **law** in a hook, a gate or branch protection?

> Anything you would be upset to discover was skipped should be a law.

That sentence resolves almost every case. You would be upset to find secrets committed, tests skipped, or the contract edited without approval — laws. You would not be upset to find a commit message in the wrong format — request.

Beware of making everything a law. Every law is friction you pay on every change, including the changes where it was unnecessary.

## Step 4: write the one page

The document you re-read before every new project:

<CodeFile title="WORKFLOW.md — one page, four sections">

```markdown
# How I work

## Rung
Start at rung 2 (one session + subagents for search).
Climb to 3 when two genuinely independent surfaces exist.
Climb to 4 when I hand-catch the same class of mistake twice.

## Crew
Default: me + one agent. Add a read-only reviewer for anything
touching auth, money or stored data formats.

## Pipeline
check.sh on every commit (lint, types, tests)
gitleaks + semgrep in pre-commit and CI
contract tests when there is more than one client
review by a different model before merge, on Beta and above

## Depths
Prototype: build only            — spikes I will delete
Alpha:     + verify it runs      — internal tools
Beta:      + tests               — anything others depend on
GA:        + second-model review — money, auth, personal data

## Always a law
No secrets committed · no --no-verify · contract changes need approval
Irreversible decisions stop and ask me
```

</CodeFile>

<Exercise id="p7c-1" title="Write your first honest WORKFLOW.md" time="60 min" predict="Which stage will you find you have been skipping for the wrong reason?">

1. List every stage you have run in this course. Mark each: always, sometimes, never.
2. For every "never", apply the upset test. Cut or fix — write which.
3. List every instruction you have typed more than twice. Turn the top four into skills.
4. Sort every rule into request or law. Move at least one thing you thought was a request.
5. Write `WORKFLOW.md` using the shape above.
6. Use it on your next task without changing it, and note every point of friction.

</Exercise>

<Expect>

Step 2 usually finds one stage you have been skipping because it is tedious rather than because it is unnecessary — most often verification. That is the highest-value automation target you have, and it was invisible until you wrote the list down.

</Expect>
